A single-seat electric helicopter, steered like a hang glider.

HELIMAC is a coaxial helicopter with an electric powertrain, designed in Bydgoszcz, Poland, to be as simple as possible: easy to transport, to fly and to maintain. The pilot steers by shifting body weight. The project is open to makers, designers, electronics people and 3D-printing fans — from school pupils to professionals. We publish drawings, calculations, software, test results and mistakes.

Inspiration: Franz Schöffmann’s backpack helicopter

HELIMAC draws on one of the simplest helicopters ever flown: the single-person machine built by Dr. Franz Schöffmann of Stockerau, Austria, flying since around 2004.

Two counter-rotating rotors above the pilot’s head, the drive right below them, and a frame of three tubes that serve as both landing gear and handles. No cabin, no tail rotor. The pilot hangs in a harness and steers by shifting body weight — just like a hang glider. Built in a home workshop, it inspired amateur builders around the world.

Sources: flight video on YouTube, “Another attempt, back-pack helicopter” thread on RotaryForum.

What we take from it

Simplicity
As few parts and mechanisms as possible. A part that isn’t there can’t fail.
Weight-shift flying
You change direction by leaning your body, intuitively, as on a hang glider.
Easy transport
A machine you can take apart and carry in an ordinary car.
A big rotor
A 4.5 m rotor spreads the weight over a large area, so hovering takes less power. The gain can go into a longer flight or a heavier pilot.
Safety
Fly low and slow, test in stages. Safety is a design goal in its own right, not an assumption.

The differences: HELIMAC has an electric powertrain instead of a combustion engine — fewer parts, less noise, no exhaust. It also has variable blade pitch set by electric actuators. On loss of power the pitch drops automatically, which by calculation allows an autorotative landing.

Design

Current design version: K (in design) · version J2 is archived – do not manufacture

Two counter-rotating rotors on one axis, no tail rotor. A single electric motor drives them through a toothed-belt drive – carbon-cord belts, no oil. The MC-P inverter and control electronics are designed in-house.

The controls are simple: the pilot changes direction by shifting body weight, because rigid hubs carry the moment from the rotors to the frame. Blade pitch (collective) is set by five electric actuators — two for the upper rotor and three for the lower, and a pitch difference between the upper and lower rotor turns the machine about its vertical axis. There is no tail rotor and no cyclic pitch.

The target is the ultralight category of Annex I to Regulation (EU) 2018/1139: a single-seat helicopter with a maximum take-off mass of up to 300 kg, which falls under national rules rather than EASA certification.

How blade pitch is set

Blade pitch is set by 5 Feetech STS3250 actuators. Each one moves all the blades of its rotor at once, never a single blade.

Upper rotor – 2 actuators

  • Both push one shared lever. The lever moves a Ø12 carbon rod running inside the column and the mast.
  • At the top of the hub the rod moves a spider, which turns both upper blades by the same angle through 2 links.
  • There are two actuators for safety: one is powered from channel A, the other from channel B. If one jams, the other changes the pitch on its own.

Lower rotor – 3 actuators

  • They push a stationary ring around the mast. Below it sits a thin-section bearing and a ring that turns with the rotor, linked to the blades by 2 links.
  • There are three because the ring needs 3 support points to slide evenly without tilting. Two are powered from channel A, one from B.

Why not each blade separately

  • HELIMAC changes only collective pitch – the same angle for all blades of a rotor. More pitch gives more thrust; less pitch gives less thrust or autorotation.
  • There is no cyclic pitch, which in a conventional helicopter tilts the rotor to steer. That job is done by the pilot shifting body weight.
  • An actuator per blade would mean actuators on the spinning hub fed through a slip ring, about 10 times faster operation (at 500 rpm the angle would change 8 times a second) and far more complex safety systems – more mass, cost and risk for no benefit.
  • Yaw comes from a pitch difference between the upper and lower rotor, set by the same actuators. No tail rotor or fin is needed.

In short: 5 actuators set just 2 angles, one per rotor. The extra actuators are a reserve in case of failure.

Compared with Jetson ONE

Jetson ONE is a flying, commercially available single-seat multicopter with eight propellers. HELIMAC has one large coaxial rotor – more than 3.5 times the rotor area of Jetson’s entire outline. That is why, by our calculations, it will fly longer on a smaller battery and carry a heavier pilot – and in version K it is also lighter.

Now: designing the drive

At this stage we are working on the mechanical and electronic design. We start with the drive — the heart of the machine — which will be tested on a dynamometer first.

Motor B

One outer-rotor motor with Halbach magnets: 30 kW continuous at 10,000 rpm, about 6 kg. Hover needs about 17 kW. The aluminium rotor cup carries fan blades; calculated efficiency about 97 %.

Belt drive 20:1

A carbon-cord toothed belt takes power from the motor to the intermediate shafts (4:1), and two final belts drive the coaxial rotor shafts (5:1). No oil, about 8.6 kg instead of about 20 kg for the version J2 gearboxes. A one-way clutch lets the rotors turn while the motor is stopped.

Reversing the direction

A 1:1 spur-gear pair on the intermediate shaft reverses the direction for one rotor, so a single motor drives both rotors and their speeds are mechanically linked.

MC-P inverter

An in-house 400 V inverter with SiC MOSFETs on a 215 × 116 mm board designed in KiCad, It fits motor B with no board changes. The heatsink is cooled by the rotor downwash; 2 fans run on the ground and as a backup.

MC-C controller

Control board (in design): motor control (FOC), fans, temperature sensors and data logging.

Dynamometer

Stage goal: the drive at full load for 30 minutes, then 50 hours of running. All results will be published.

The whole machine

Two contra-rotating Ø 4.5 m rotors at 500 rpm, one motor and a belt drive above the pilot’s head. Frame of three 7075-T6 aluminium tubes: two front legs forming a triangle and a rear leg bent towards the legs, carrying the seat, footrest and 40 kg battery. These views come from the 23 Nov model – since 26 Nov the motor sits upside down above the belts and the drive ratio is 20:1.

Motor B – 30 kW, 10,000 rpm

Outer-rotor motor: a rotating 7075 aluminium cup with fan blades surrounds the stationary wound stator. The magnets form a Halbach array (4 segments per pole) that focuses the field inwards, so the rotor needs no steel back iron. 18 slots, 16 poles, Ø 214 mm, 24 mm lamination stack, 192 N48SH magnets 9 × 6 × 7.9 mm, 9 turns per coil, 2 × 6206 bearings. Mass about 6 kg (previous motor 7.1 kg). FEA report (PDF), motor and rotor data sheet (PDF)

How we calculate the motor

We compute the motor’s magnetic field with the finite element method (FEA):

  1. The motor cross-section – stator laminations, 18 coils and 192 magnets – is split into small triangles, densest in the 1 mm air gap between the teeth and the magnets, where the torque is made.
  2. The solver computes the field in every triangle, including the saturation of the 0.2 mm NO20 steel (its B-H curve) and the weakening of the magnets at operating temperature.
  3. We repeat this for 25 rotor positions. Without current we get the induced voltage and cogging torque; with current, the shaft torque and its ripple.

Result for motor B: 28.5 N·m at 84 A (30 kW at 10,000 rpm), torque ripple 2.2 %, cogging torque about 0.9 N·m, losses about 810 W, efficiency about 97 %. About 48 A in hover.

Rotor and blades

We compared 13 aerofoils, each at 380–620 rpm with various chords and twists (566 variants). Choice: the VR-7 aerofoil with a trailing-edge tab bent 4° up – the tab cuts the blade pitching moment almost threefold. The optimum is flat (470–530 rpm); we keep 500 rpm, 118 m/s tip speed. Compared with the NACA 23012: about 3 % less power and about 1.4 min longer flight.

Blade: 150 mm chord out to 1 m from the axis, then tapering to 102 mm; −8° twist; mass 2.04 kg. Foam core, “D” spar of unidirectional carbon (up to 12 layers), ±45° carbon fabric skin, 0.665 kg tungsten tip mass (energy for autorotation). Prototype: XPS/EPS core hot-wire cut from templates; the upper-rotor blades mirror the lower ones. blade drawings (PDF)

20:1 belt drive

Carbon-cord toothed belts (8M), two stages: 24/96 teeth (4:1), then 2 × 24/120 teeth (5:1). Motor 10,000 → intermediate shafts 2500 → rotors 500 rpm. A gear pair reverses one shaft, so the rotors turn in opposite directions, always at the same speed. The motor sits upside down above the belts, 212 mm from the rotor axis. A one-way clutch lets the rotors autorotate while the motor is stopped. Mass 8.55 kg, 0 collisions in the 3D model. Belt widths will be confirmed with the manufacturer’s software. belt drive drawings (PDF)

Blade pitch control

The rotors are linked by belts, so they always turn at the same speed. Thrust and yaw are controlled only by blade pitch – two tasks need two independent angles, so both rotors have variable pitch. Mechanical range −2…+15° (at 3/4 blade length), hover 8.9° (upper) and 9.7° (lower), autorotation about 2.7°. 5 STS3250 actuators: 2 for the upper rotor (on the frame below the pulley), 3 for the lower (on the spider above the belts). Design pitch-link force 114 N; a 7° pitch change takes about 0.07 s. The pitch plates only slide vertically (±9 mm) – they do not tilt, as there is no cyclic control. 3D check with motion: 0 collisions, smallest clearance 9.5 mm. SK (PDF), MS (PDF), TS (PDF)

Drawings SK-01 and SK-02 still show a 100 mm joint radius; the correct value is 116.6 mm at 31° (as in the MS and TS drawings).

Hub and blade attachment

The blade turns (pitch change) on a spindle that is one piece with the 7075 aluminium hub body. Bearings: 2 needle bearings and a thrust bearing that carries the centrifugal force – 7.4 kN at 500 rpm, 9.8 kN at 575 rpm. The blade sits in a fork on 2 Ø12 pins, removable in about 5 min. No elastomers in the bearings. 4.3° precone: in hover the blade root hardly bends. Mass 1.9 kg per rotor. hub drawings (PDF)

MC-P inverter

Our own design, rev D1: 6 SiC 1200 V transistors, DESAT short-circuit protection, 400 V, 215 × 116 mm board. It fits motor B with no board changes; only the current sensors (±150 A) and the firmware (16 poles, 32 kHz switching, field weakening) change. Hover: 48 A, 210 W losses, junction about 78 °C; 1.3 g on a hot day: 74 A, briefly about 127 °C. Heatsink with vertical fins in the rotor downwash (9–10 m/s), plus 2 fans for ground running and as a backup. Mounted on the battery box behind the rear leg; 2.23 kg with cables. inverter drawings (PDF)

Designed for safety

We design HELIMAC so that a single fault does not end in an accident. Every assembly is calculated with a margin and tested on a bench before it goes into the machine.

Strength margins

Blades, hubs, frame and seat are sized for ultimate loads (load factor × 1.5). For example:

  • blade – the 7.4 kN centrifugal force (9.8 kN at 575 rpm) is carried by a thrust bearing in the hub;
  • frame – front legs: buckling margin 2.9 in a 3 g landing;
  • stationary rotor column – margin 11.

Vibration

Blade natural frequencies stay well away from resonance across the whole speed range (flap 1.25/rev, in-plane about 1.4/rev for the 23 Nov blades; the new 150/102 mm blades will be checked before building, torsion above 10/rev). Rotors and motor are balanced to grade G2.5.

Belt drive

Belts with a load margin of at least 1.5, no oil. Speed sensors on both rotors: a broken belt shuts the motor down and starts autorotation. Belts are checked before every flight; hub bearings last about 12,000 h.

400 V electrics

  • Fuse and contactor with soft pre-charge of the capacitors.
  • Isolation between the high-voltage side and the controls.
  • 1.5 kV insulation test before the first start.
  • On a short circuit the inverter transistors switch off within a fraction of a microsecond.

Two independent control channels

Channels A and B each have their own supply from one of the two battery packs, their own drivers and actuators. They monitor each other, and a third angle sensor decides which one is right. A jammed actuator disconnects through an overload clutch and the others carry on alone.

Check before every flight

Each actuator runs through its full travel on its own. A current difference above 15 % blocks take-off.

Autorotation – what happens when power is lost

When power is lost, rotor speed falls from 500 to 425 rpm in about 0.45 s. That is too fast for the pilot, so an automatic system does it. The controller detects the drop in motor current and speed and immediately lowers the blade pitch of both rotors to about 2.7° (at 3/4 blade length); the actuators change pitch by 7° in about 0.07 s. Aerodynamic and centrifugal forces themselves reduce blade pitch, so if the control fails the blades move towards autorotation. The rotors go into autorotation: the air flowing up through the rotor keeps them turning.

Rate of descent (calculated):

  • at 16 m/s forward speed – about 6 m/s (pilot drag included);
  • straight down, with no forward speed – about 12–13 m/s, too fast to land.

Near the ground the pilot raises blade pitch (the flare). The rotors’ stored energy brakes the descent.

The zone we avoid. As in any helicopter, hovering is safe only up to about 5 m above the ground or above about 150 m. In between there is neither the time nor the height to pick up speed and enter autorotation.

Handling – how quickly the machine responds

Climb and descent
Changing blade pitch changes thrust in about 0.2 s. Changing thrust through rotor speed would take 2–3 s.
Yaw
A ±0.6° pitch difference between the upper and lower rotor gives about ±40 N·m of torque, so no tail rotor is needed.
Direction
The pilot shifts body weight, as on a hang glider. At 25 m/s that is about 27 cm forward.
Top speed
About 25 m/s (90 km/h). Above that the nose-up moment and blade vibration grow.
Precision
Actuator encoders resolve 0.09° of rotation, about 0.01 mm of travel at the blade lever.

How we calculate

All values are calculated today, not measured. We publish the methods and scripts so anyone can check them:

  • rotors – blade element momentum theory (BEM) including the effect of the upper rotor on the lower one;
  • motor – finite element analysis (FEA) of the magnetic field and a thermal balance;
  • gearing – Hertz contact stress, L10 bearing life, oil temperature;
  • blades – composite ply strength, natural frequencies, torsional divergence;
  • frame – strength and buckling of the carbon tubes, centre of gravity.

Every number will be verified by tests: the dynamometer (full load for 30 min, then 50 h), tethered rotor tests and uncrewed flights.

A review of our calculations by experienced engineers is the most valuable help we can get.

The figures apply to version K (take-off mass about 193 kg) and are calculated; an autorotation simulation and tests will confirm them. The frame will also be checked with finite element analysis (FEA).

From HeliMac to HELIMAC

2013–2015

The name combines helicopter and Maciąg. Tomasz Maciąg, a hands-on engineer with many years in refrigeration and automation, worked on HeliMac in 2013–2015: a concept for a single-person electric vertical take-off vehicle. He built a team, ran powertrain tests and worked with, among others, Military Aviation Works No. 2 in Bydgoszcz and the KOMEL Institute.

Funded mostly from private savings, the project never found an investor. In hindsight it was simply too early: Jetson was founded in 2017, several years after HeliMac had been covered by the Polish media. Interestingly, Jetson has Polish roots too: co-founder Tomasz Patan built and flew the first prototype in Poland. HeliMac had been featured on Polish radio, TV and the internet a few years earlier — we don’t know whether Jetson’s founders came across it, but such ideas were in the air and may have inspired others.

Why now

Today's HELIMAC continues that project, but it is a new design. The difference is artificial intelligence: work that in 2015 had to be split among many paid specialists can now be carried by one person with the help of AI tools.

Safety first

A 4.5 m rotor, a 400 V battery and an uncertified design are three independent sources of lethal risk. Nothing in this project is yet a verified, buildable design. Everything is provided without any warranty.

Rules for testing, high-voltage work and reporting problems: SAFETY.md.

Who HELIMAC is for

For everyone who enjoys building things: makers, designers, electronics people and 3D-printing fans — professionals and hobbyists, school pupils and university students too. Everyone can learn something and contribute something.

Electronics and software

The SiC inverter, the MC-C control board, sensors, BMS and software. KiCad projects, code on GitHub.

Designers and engineers

CAD, strength calculations, rotors, batteries, regulations. Reviewing and challenging our calculations is the most valuable help.
